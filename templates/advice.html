Advice & Guidance for Matric Students

General guidance to help you through matric, choosing a course, and your first year at university.

Choosing a Course

  • Start with what you're genuinely interested in and good at, not just what pays well or what your friends are choosing.
  • Research what the actual day-to-day work in that career looks like, not just the job title.
  • Check the specific subject and APS requirements for that course at each university you're considering - they vary a lot.
  • If you're unsure, a general/broad degree (like a BA or BCom) can keep more doors open than a highly specialised one.
  • Talk to people who are actually studying or working in that field if you can.

Studying for Your Matric Exams

  • Past papers are one of the most effective tools you have - they show you exactly how questions are asked.
  • Spread your revision out over weeks rather than cramming in the last few days - your brain retains information better this way.
  • Study in shorter, focused sessions with breaks, rather than long unfocused ones.
  • Teach the material to someone else (or even out loud to yourself) - it quickly reveals what you don't actually understand yet.
  • Prioritise sleep, especially before exam days - it matters more for memory and focus than an extra hour of late-night cramming.

Taking Care of Yourself

  • Matric is genuinely stressful for almost everyone - you are not alone in finding it hard.
  • Try to keep some normal routine going: meals, sleep, and a bit of time away from studying each day.
  • Talk to someone - a parent, teacher, school counsellor, or friend - if the pressure is becoming too much to carry alone.
  • Your matric results matter, but they do not define your worth or determine the rest of your life on their own.

What to Expect at University

  • The workload and pace are faster than school, and no one will chase you to catch up - it's on you to manage your own time.
  • Classes are often much bigger, and lecturers won't necessarily know your name the way teachers did.
  • Budgeting matters - food, transport, printing, and data add up fast. Plan for it before you arrive if you can.
  • It's normal to feel lost or overwhelmed in your first few weeks. Almost everyone does - it settles down.
  • Use your university's orientation week properly - it's designed to help you find your feet, not just a formality.

Watch Out for Scams

Application and registration scams increase every year around matric results season. Real, documented warning signs from universities and education authorities include:

  • Anyone contacting you on social media claiming to guarantee you admission or a residence spot for a fee.
  • Being asked to pay money urgently, especially in cash, or through unofficial channels.
  • Colleges or institutions that cannot show proof of registration with the Department of Higher Education and Training (DHET), or that use vague terms like "internationally recognised" without a clear NQF registration.
  • Pressure to pay before you've even seen an official offer letter from the university itself.

Always apply and check your status only through a university's official website, or verify a college's registration through SAQA's National Learners' Records Database before paying anyone, anything.

Bursaries You Can Look Into

These are well-known, legitimate bursary programmes in South Africa. This is not an application service - search for each one directly on its own official website to check requirements and apply.

  • NSFAS (National Student Financial Aid Scheme)
  • Funza Lushaka Bursary (for teaching qualifications)
  • Sasol Bursary (engineering, science, IT, commerce)
  • Allan Gray Orbis Foundation Fellowship
  • SAICA Thuthuka Bursary (accounting)
  • Investec Bursary
  • Transnet Bursary
  • Nedbank Bursary
  • IDC External Bursary
  • Mandela Rhodes Scholarship
  • Eskom Bursary
  • Provincial government bursaries (varies by province)
